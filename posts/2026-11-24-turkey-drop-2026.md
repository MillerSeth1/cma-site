Every Thanksgiving, a lot of us come back to the same half-hour of television: *WKRP in Cincinnati* and the radio promotion that did not go as planned. From the ramp at Nogales International Airport (KOLS), it still makes a pretty good aviation story.

“Turkeys Away,” season 1, episode 7, first aired on CBS on October 30, 1978. It was written by Bill Dial, directed by Michael Zinberg, and created, like the rest of the series, by Hugh Wilson. In 1997, *TV Guide* ranked it No. 40 on its list of the 100 Greatest Episodes of All Time.

## The greatest Thanksgiving promotion in radio history

Feeling left out at the station, manager Arthur Carlson — “the Big Guy,” played by Gordon Jump — hatches a secret Thanksgiving stunt with sales manager Herb Tarlek (Frank Bonner). Newsman Les Nessman (Richard Sanders) is sent to the fictional Pinedale Shopping Mall for a remote broadcast. A helicopter appears, towing a banner that reads “Happy Thanksgiving from WKRP.” Then objects start falling from the back. Nessman first wonders if they might be skydivers. They are not. They are live turkeys.

In a send-up of Herbert Morrison’s 1937 *Hindenburg* report, Nessman tells listeners the birds are hitting the ground “like sacks of wet cement,” and even uses Morrison’s line, “Oh, the humanity!” No turkeys appear on screen; the drop lives in the report and in the audience’s imagination. When Carlson walks back into the station, covered in feathers, he delivers the line that has followed the show ever since:

> “As God is my witness, I thought turkeys could fly.”

## A real promotion, with the details in dispute

Series creator Hugh Wilson has said the story was inspired by a tale from Jerry Blum, the general manager of Atlanta’s WQXI, the station that also inspired many of *WKRP*’s characters. Accounts differ. Blum’s family and *The Atlanta Journal-Constitution* have described turkeys tossed from a pickup truck at a Dallas shopping-center parking lot earlier in Blum’s career. Wilson later recalled Blum describing a helicopter. Either way, the punch line was the same: the turkeys did not fly.

## An aviation lesson, the gentle way

Mr. Carlson is not entirely wrong. Wild turkeys can fly. The National Park Service notes they have been clocked at up to about 55 miles an hour in short bursts. The broad-breasted birds raised for a Thanksgiving table are another matter. Selective breeding has left them with large breast muscles and short wings, and they are, as the Park Service puts it, “virtually flightless.” A helicopter is not how you deliver either one.

Dropping objects from an aircraft is also regulated. Under [14 CFR 91.15](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-F/part-91/subpart-A/section-91.15), a pilot in command may not allow any object to be dropped from a civil aircraft in flight that creates a hazard to persons or property. The rule does not ban every drop, but it does require reasonable precautions to avoid injury or damage. A live-turkey giveaway over a crowded parking lot would fail that test. We’ll leave the turkeys on the ground.
